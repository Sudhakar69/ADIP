Incident queue — Monitoring & Response

MON-401SEV-1Production is down. You get paged. What are your first five minutes?▶
  • Acknowledge the page (stop it re-escalating) and open the relevant dashboard — don't start guessing from memory.
  • Confirm blast radius: one region, all regions, one service, or a shared dependency (DNS, auth, database)?
  • Declare an incident with a severity and, if the org has one, an incident commander role — communication starts now, not after you've found root cause.
  • Look for what changed recently: deploys, config, feature flags, infra changes in the last few hours — most incidents trace back to a recent change.

Resolution

Mitigate first: rollback, feature-flag off, or scale up/failover — restore service before you fully understand why. Root cause analysis happens after the fire is out.

MON-402SEV-2Your team is getting paged 40 times a week and burning out. How do you fix alerting, not just tolerance for pages?▶
  • Audit every alert: does it map to actual user-facing impact (SLO burn rate), or is it a raw resource threshold (CPU > 80%) that's frequently noisy and not actionable?
  • Convert "page-worthy" to symptom-based alerts (error rate, latency, availability) and demote cause-based signals (disk 70% full) to tickets/dashboards, not pages.
  • Add alert grouping/deduplication and sensible for-durations so one blip doesn't fire five pages.

Resolution

Every alert should be actionable and tied to user impact. If an alert fires and the response is "nothing to do," it shouldn't be a page.

MON-403SEV-3Define SLI, SLO, and error budget, and how they change your deploy decisions.▶
  • SLI (indicator): a measured metric, e.g. "% of requests under 300ms."
  • SLO (objective): a target for that SLI, e.g. "99.9% of requests under 300ms over 30 days."
  • Error budget: the allowed 0.1% — once it's spent, the team's priority shifts from shipping features to reliability work, and risky deploys get paused or gated behind extra review.
observability
MON-404SEV-1Prometheus itself is down / not scraping — you're flying blind during an incident. What do you do?▶

Fall back to secondary signals: cloud provider platform metrics (Azure Monitor), application logs directly, or a synthetic uptime check that's independent of your primary stack. This is also the argument for why monitoring infrastructure itself needs redundancy/HA and shouldn't share a failure domain with what it's monitoring — e.g. don't run your only Prometheus inside the same cluster it's the sole observer of, without a secondary path.

MON-405SEV-2Walk me through writing a blameless postmortem after a major outage.▶
  • Timeline of objective facts: when it started, when detected, when mitigated, when resolved — timestamps, not opinions.
  • Impact: quantify it (users affected, revenue, SLO burn) rather than "it was bad."
  • Root cause via 5 Whys, focused on systems and process gaps, never on naming/blaming an individual.
  • Action items: specific, owned, dated, and tracked to closure — a postmortem with no completed action items is theater.
i

Language matters: "the deploy process allowed an unvalidated config to reach prod," not "Dave pushed bad config." Interviewers listen for this framing specifically.

MON-406SEV-2Disk usage on a node is climbing steadily and will hit 100% in 6 hours. How do you handle it — page now or wait?▶

This is a textbook case for a warning-level alert with a longer response window, not a page — it's predictable and not yet impacting users. Route it to a ticket/Slack channel with a clear runbook (log rotation, old artifact cleanup, or scale storage), and only escalate to a page if the trend accelerates or crosses a near-term threshold (e.g. <1 hour to full).

MON-408SEV-2Latency degraded slowly over two weeks, no single deploy caused it, and nobody noticed until customers complained. What failed?▶

The alerting was threshold-based on a moving baseline that crept along with the degradation ("boiling frog" problem), or there was no SLO-based burn-rate alert at all — only "is it fully down" checks. Fix: alert on rate of change and on absolute SLO thresholds together, and review trend dashboards on a regular cadence, not only reactively.

MON-409SEV-1During an active outage, three engineers are independently trying different fixes and stepping on each other. What's wrong with the response and how do you fix it live?▶

Missing incident command structure. Assign a single Incident Commander who coordinates and makes the final call on which mitigation to try (and in what order), a dedicated communications channel/thread for status updates, and route all changes through the IC so actions aren't taken in parallel without visibility. This is a process question as much as a technical one — seniority shows in recognizing coordination failure as the actual root cause.

MON-410SEV-2How do you monitor a Kubernetes cluster itself, separate from the applications running on it?▶
  • kube-state-metrics for object-level state (deployment replica mismatches, pod phase counts, PVC status).
  • node-exporter (DaemonSet) for host-level CPU/mem/disk/network per node.
  • cAdvisor (built into kubelet) for per-container resource usage.
  • Control-plane metrics (API server latency, etcd health) — critical on self-managed clusters, mostly abstracted away on AKS.
kubernetesobservability
MON-411SEV-2Logs from 40 microservices are unsearchable — everyone greps individual pod logs. What's your fix?▶

Centralize: ship every container's stdout/stderr via a DaemonSet log agent (Fluent Bit) to a central store (ELK or Log Analytics), enforce structured JSON logging with a correlation/trace ID on every log line, so a single request can be followed across services with one query instead of forty separate kubectl logs commands.

MON-412SEV-3How do you balance "move fast" with "keep things reliable" — the JD literally asks for this balance.▶

Error budgets are the concrete mechanism: as long as the budget isn't exhausted, teams ship freely; once it is, velocity yields to reliability work automatically, by policy rather than by argument. Pair that with progressive delivery (canary/feature flags) so "moving fast" doesn't mean "betting the whole fleet on every change" — blast radius is controlled independently of release frequency.
